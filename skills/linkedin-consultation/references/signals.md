# Signals: Skills, Recommendations, Open to work, extras

## Skills

LinkedIn allows dozens of skills, but in my experience the top 3 are the ones that carry
algorithmic weight. Someone targeting a Senior Automations role whose top skills are Public
Speaking and Microsoft Word will rank lower than they should.

**Three distinct features, often confused:**

- **Skills section** — the main store for everything across a career. Past a certain number,
  LinkedIn sorts them into categories.
- **Top Skills** — the 5 shown before the fold. The only pre-click signal a human sees.
- **Role-related skills** — assigned per role inside the experience section.

Top 3 and Top 5 are not a contradiction: 3 is about algorithmic weight, 5 is about what a human
sees before scrolling. Both matter, for different reasons.

**What to do:**
- **Reorder.** Most relevant technical skills and expertise to the top.
- **Assign.** Attach specific skills to specific roles in experience. This builds a skill map
  that both recruiters and automated tools can read.
- **Endorsements** are worth nurturing. Encourage endorsing colleagues for skills genuinely
  valued; it tends to come back.

## Recommendations

> Treat recommendations like a peer performance review. Generic questions produce only generic
> feedback.

Specificity equals credibility. Guide the user to request narrow ones:

1. **Set the scene.** Remind the person of a specific project, initiative or timeframe.
2. **Give them a focus.** Ask them to speak to specific skills or outcomes: by doing X we
   achieved Y.
3. **Give them an out.** Always let them say no, or defer.
4. **Ease the load.** Offer a short draft as a starting point to save their time.

> "Hey, could you write 3 sentences about how I handled the migration project last Q3?"

## Open to work

Two settings, and they are a real trade-off rather than an on/off switch.

**Recruiter-only.** Visible to LinkedIn Recruiter seat holders, not on the public profile. Right
for anyone employed and searching discreetly, or anyone who doesn't want the signal attached to
their public presence. The trade-off: it only reaches recruiters actively using Recruiter, so
reach is narrower.

**Public `#OpenToWork` banner.** Visible to everyone, including the user's network and their
current employer. Wider reach, and it makes the ask explicit so a first-degree connection can
act on it without having to guess. The trade-off: it is public, permanently visible while on,
and some hiring managers read it as a signal of urgency. That reading is not universal and is
much weaker than it used to be, but it exists.

**How to advise:** ask whether they are employed, whether their search is discreet, and whether
their network is a realistic source of leads. Employed and discreet points to recruiter-only.
Between roles with an active network points to the public banner. Neither is a default; the
point is that it should be a decision rather than whatever it happens to be set to.

Also check the field is actually filled in properly: job titles, location types and start
availability feed the same filters recruiters search on.

## Extras, light pass

- **Education and licences.** Use logos. Keep certifications current; an expired one is worse
  than none.
- **Volunteering.** Signals the user is a person, not just a CV. Worth including if genuine.
- **Languages.** Proficiency level matters. It may be the reason a profile makes a recruiter's
  filter cut.

> 🛑 Reality check: 500+ connections isn't a vanity metric, it signals someone is plugged in. My
> rule of thumb from practice: 20 mutual connections makes a profile roughly 5x more likely to be
> trusted, because there's someone to ask about the person. Worth growing and maintaining a
> network year-round, not only at job-search time.
