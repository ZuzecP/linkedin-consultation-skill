# Narrative: About, Featured, Experience

## About

Write this section **last**, after experience is solid. It's the narrative layer on top of the
facts, and it can only be written well once the facts are clear.

> The About section is where you tell the "why" behind the "what". Don't just list skills, weave
> a narrative.

Careers are becoming less linear. This section draws the reader a clear picture of how to read
the rest of the profile.

- **Optimise for attention.** Most important information first, then expand. Think of it as a
  press release opening: the key point is in the first sentence, not buried at the end.
- **Tone of voice.** Consistent across the whole profile. This is where genuine personality
  belongs.
- **Focus on the future.** What is the next career move? And what is the user explicitly *not*
  interested in? Stating both lets recruiters self-qualify, which saves everyone time.
- **Transferable skills.** What carries across roles. Most important for non-linear careers.
- **Personality markers.** Gallup/CliftonStrengths or MBTI can work if genuinely relevant to work
  style. They give a hook for the first conversation with a recruiter or hiring manager.

## Featured

This is the portfolio. Select the best and most relevant work, activities and projects.

- Developer → GitHub, personal site
- Marketer → Medium article, case study
- HR → a deck, framework, or initiative write-up
- Public speaker → a post about the event, with photos

Aim for 3+ items in the carousel. Prioritisation matters more than quantity.

> 🤖 On AI sourcing: my read on this is that a profile which is only text gets classified as an
> average candidate, while a Featured section carrying external links (Behance, GitHub, Medium)
> pushes it into expert-level match territory. It's the difference between page 1 and page 10 of
> a search. My observation from practice, not a published statistic.

## Experience

This section requires the most rigour. **Do not generate copy until you have evidence.**

Most people write their experience section like a job description. Recruiters already know what
the role requires. They want to know what *you* did.

### Before writing anything, for each role

Ask these in sequence, one at a time or batched by role. Do not move to drafting until you have
real answers.

1. What did you actually *do* in this role? Not the job description, the specific things you
   worked on or led.
2. What was the measurable outcome or impact? Numbers, percentages, scale, anything concrete.
3. What tools, methods or approaches were specific to how you did it?
4. If the answers are vague, push back. Keep asking until you have specifics.

**Follow-ups when answers stay soft:**
- "How would someone have noticed if you hadn't done it?"
- "What was it like before you started, and after?"
- "Who else was involved, and what was your part specifically?"
- "How many? Over what period? Compared to what?"

### Then, and only then

**The Golden Formula:** `[Action Verb] + [Quantifiable Result] + [Method/Tool]`

- ❌ "Responsible for managing the dev team."
- ✅ "Led a cross-functional squad of 8 to reduce API latency by 40% using Node.js and Redis
  caching."

> Take it with a grain of salt. Not every sentence has to sound like that. Stay genuine and true
> to the user's tone of voice. But if they cringe a bit reading their own description, good.
> That's what we're aiming for.

### If the user declines the exercise

If they say "skip this, just polish the wording": don't insist. Say plainly that you can polish
the existing text, but without specifics the result will be phrasing improvements only, with no
added impact or evidence. Then do exactly that, and invent nothing.

### Formatting

- Make the first two lines stand out. They're visible before the "see more" cut.
- Use LinkedIn media chips (links, images, documents). Clean visual plus a caption for each.
- Structure by projects, agendas, or areas of expertise where that fits the role.
- Use dividers: special characters and symbols, not only emoji.

### Gaps and freelancing

- Don't hide career breaks. Use LinkedIn's Career Break feature, for health and wellbeing or
  caregiving. It signals transparency, not weakness.
- Gaps of one to two months don't need an entry, they'd only pad the profile.
- Freelance work: group under one heading, list notable projects underneath.

> 🛑 Reality check: recruiters do click the media links in the experience section. If the user
> mentions a successful product launch, ask whether they can attach the press release or a UI
> screenshot. It's evidence, not decoration.
